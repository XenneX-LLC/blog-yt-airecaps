Thanksgiving is November 26 this year. If you're hosting, or you've been assigned a side dish, you're about to watch a lot of cooking videos.

Most of them follow the same shape. A few minutes of the host's kitchen and their week. A story about their grandmother. A sponsor read. Then the actual recipe, somewhere past the seven-minute mark, moving faster than you can write it down.

That's fine when you're in the mood to watch. It's a lot less fine when you're comparing six stuffing recipes on a Tuesday night trying to decide which one to make.

## Recipe Videos Aren't Like Other Videos

We've written before about why [lectures and talks are the easiest videos to summarize](/blog/lectures-and-talks-easiest-to-summarize). A recipe video is a different kind of easy, and a different kind of hard.

The easy part: what you want from it is clear. You want to know what goes in, in what order, and what the cook says you can't skip. There's no plot to spoil and no argument to follow.

The hard part: a recipe is a sequence. If a summary turns "brown the butter, then add the sage" into a nice paragraph about how the dish "builds layers of flavor," it hasn't helped you. The order is the whole point.

## What AI Recaps Does With a Recipe

AI Recaps doesn't have a special recipe mode. It has a rule for tutorials, and a recipe video is a tutorial: when a video is teaching a process, the summary is structured around the steps instead of the story.

So for a typical cooking video you get:

- **A TL;DR** that tells you what the dish is and what makes this version different. Dry brine or wet brine. Cornbread or white bread. Make-ahead or day-of.
- **Key points** with the specifics: temperatures, times, and the warnings the cook spends a full minute on.
- **The method, in order**, without the grandmother story in between.

Sponsor reads get pulled out of the summary itself and listed separately at the end, so the meal kit ad doesn't end up in the middle of your gravy.

## Where It Falls Short

This part matters, so we'll be plain about it.

AI Recaps works from what's said in the video. If the cook says "two cups of broth," that's in the summary. If they just pour from a measuring cup while the amount flashes on screen, the summary won't know it was two cups.

A lot of cooking channels put the exact quantities in the video description or on a linked blog post instead of saying them out loud. For those, use the summary to choose the recipe and get the method, then grab the measurements from the description before you start.

The same goes for technique you need to see. A summary can tell you to fold the dough over itself. It can't show you what "until it looks shaggy" looks like. When the cook's hands are the lesson, skip to that part of the video and watch it. It's the same reason [YouTube chapters aren't a summary](/blog/youtube-chapters-arent-a-summary): one tells you where things are, the other tells you what they say, and some moments you still have to see.

## How to Use It This Month

**Shortlist fast.** Search for the dish, save the five or six videos that look promising, and read the summaries. Two minutes per video instead of twenty, and you'll know which ones are worth watching in full.

**Compare versions side by side.** The TL;DRs make the differences obvious. One turkey is spatchcocked, one is roasted whole, one goes in a bag. Pick the one that fits your oven and your schedule.

**Watch the one you're making.** Once you've picked, watch that video properly, at least the parts with technique. The summary gets you to the right video. It doesn't replace standing in the kitchen with it.

**Save the summary.** If you're signed in, it stays in My Summaries, so on Thanksgiving morning you have the steps in order without scrubbing through the intro with buttery fingers.

## The Bottom Line

A recipe video is a great way to learn a dish and a slow way to pick one. Let the summary do the picking, check the description for exact amounts, and save your watching for the recipe you're actually going to cook.
