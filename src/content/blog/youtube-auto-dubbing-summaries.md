You click a video from a creator in another country, and they're speaking your language. Their mouth doesn't quite match the words, and there's a small "auto-dubbed" label in the description.

That's YouTube's automatic dubbing. In February, YouTube made it available to all creators in 27 languages. It said more than 6 million people a day were watching at least 10 minutes of auto-dubbed content back in December. When a creator uploads a video, YouTube can now generate dubbed audio tracks for it on its own.

It's a useful feature. It also raises a fair question if you summarize what you watch: is the summary built on the dub?

## What a Dub Actually Is

An auto dub is a new audio track. YouTube transcribes what the speaker said, translates it, and has a synthetic voice read the translation. Viewers can switch between the dub and the original with the audio track selector, or set a preferred language so they get the original by default.

YouTube is open about the limits. Its help page says dubs "might contain errors due to mispronunciations, accents, dialects, or background noise in the original video," and that it can have trouble with "proper nouns, idioms, and jargon." Creators can't edit an automatic dub. They can only turn it off, regenerate it, or replace it with their own.

So by the time you hear a dub, the words have been through speech recognition, translation and a synthetic voice. Each step can lose something. A joke that depends on wordplay doesn't survive translation. A product name gets translated when it shouldn't. A speaker's sarcasm comes out flat.

## Why the Summary Doesn't Start From the Dub

AI Recaps doesn't listen to the audio you're hearing. It starts from the video's captions.

It looks for an English caption track first. If there isn't one, and the video has Spanish or French captions, it uses those instead. The summary is then written in the same language as the captions it read. Choosing the dubbed audio track in the player doesn't change any of that.

For a video recorded in English, that means the summary comes from the creator's own words, not a translation of them. That's the main reason the dubbing rollout doesn't change much for summaries.

There's one exception. If a video has no captions at all, AI Recaps can fall back to transcribing the audio itself. That's the one case where the audio matters, and it's the same case where audio quality matters most. We wrote about that in [what happens with bad audio](/blog/what-happens-with-bad-audio).

## What to Watch For

A few habits help when dubbing is involved:

- **Check for the label.** If the description says "auto-dubbed," the voice you're hearing isn't the creator's. Quotes from the dub aren't quotes from the creator.
- **Be careful with exact wording.** Names, numbers and technical terms are where translation goes wrong most often. If a detail matters, check it in the original language.
- **Know what the summary read.** If a video only has Spanish or French captions, the summary will be in Spanish or French too.

## What's Next

YouTube isn't done. It has been piloting lip sync, which changes the speaker's mouth movements to match the dubbed audio. At Made on YouTube in September, it said it will start piloting real-time dubbing for livestreams in early 2027.

More dubbed video means more chances to hear something that isn't quite what was said. YouTube's own AI tools have limits too, which we covered in [what YouTube's AI still doesn't do](/blog/youtube-ai-what-it-still-doesnt-do). A summary built from captions doesn't solve that, but it gives you something closer to the original to check against.
