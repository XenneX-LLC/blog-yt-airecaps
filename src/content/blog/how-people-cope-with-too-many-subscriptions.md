Ask around and everyone with more than a handful of subscriptions has some coping strategy for the volume. None of them are formal systems — they're habits people fell into out of necessity, and almost none of them are actually working. Here's an honest look at the strategies people actually use, and why each one fails in a specific, predictable way.

## Strategy One: Watch Everything at Higher Speed

The most common response to too much video is to speed up, not cut back. 1.5x becomes 2x becomes, for some people, whatever the app's ceiling is. This treats the problem as a throughput issue — if the bottleneck is time, more speed should fix it.

**Why it doesn't work:** speed-watching doesn't actually solve the volume problem, it just makes each individual video worse while the total time spent stays roughly the same, because the backlog grows faster than any sustainable speed increase can offset. And comprehension genuinely degrades past a certain point — you're not getting the same video faster, you're getting a worse version of it in less time, which is a different trade than most people think they're making.

## Strategy Two: Let the Unwatched Count Climb and Ignore It

This is the passive version — stop trying to keep up, let the number in the corner of the subscriptions tab climb into the hundreds, and mentally treat it as noise instead of a to-do list.

**Why it doesn't work:** ignoring the count doesn't remove the guilt, it just changes when the guilt shows up. It resurfaces every time you open the app and see the number, and it resurfaces hard when you eventually decide to "catch up," discover the scale of what's accumulated, and give up before starting. The count doesn't actually become invisible. It just becomes a low-grade background irritant instead of an active task.

## Strategy Three: Unsubscribe Aggressively

Some people respond to overload by periodically purging subscriptions — cutting the list down hard, on the theory that fewer sources means a manageable volume.

**Why it doesn't work, at least not cleanly:** this solves the volume problem by discarding channels you actually liked, not just the ones you'd outgrown. The purge is usually based on which channels feel guilty to see unwatched, which correlates poorly with which channels you'd actually still want if the backlog problem weren't in the way. People regularly unsubscribe from something they'd have genuinely enjoyed, purely because the guilt of the backlog made the channel itself feel like a burden.

## Strategy Four: Pick One "Main" Platform and Ignore Everything Else

A newer pattern: consolidate down to whichever single platform or format feels most manageable, and mentally write off content that lives anywhere else, regardless of whether it's actually good.

**Why it doesn't work:** it optimizes for administrative simplicity over actual content quality, which means you're systematically missing things you'd value, sorted by which platform they happen to live on rather than whether they're worth your time. The constraint is arbitrary relative to the actual goal.

## What All Four Have in Common

Every one of these strategies treats the problem as needing to be solved at the volume level — watch it all faster, ignore the volume, cut the volume, or artificially cap the volume. None of them actually address the real bottleneck, which isn't how much content exists. It's that deciding what's worth your time requires watching enough of something to know, and that decision cost is the actual constraint, not the raw number of videos.

## The Alternative: Move the Decision Point, Not the Volume

The fix isn't managing volume more aggressively. It's making the "is this worth my time" decision cheaper, so you can apply it to everything without the exhaustion that makes people default to one of the four broken strategies above. A summary does exactly this — it's not a smaller pile of content, it's a faster way to sort the pile you already have into what's actually worth full attention and what isn't, without needing to unsubscribe from anything, speed anything up, or pretend the count doesn't exist.

## The Bottom Line

The coping strategies people actually use for subscription overload all fail for the same underlying reason: they try to shrink or ignore the volume instead of making the sorting decision itself faster. Once sorting is cheap, the volume stops being the problem it currently is — you don't need fewer subscriptions, you need a faster way to know which ones are worth this specific video.
