The US Open starts next week, and the actual storylines worth knowing walking in — who's peaking, who's a genuine dark horse, what changed since the last major — are scattered across weeks of warm-up tournament coverage, preview shows, and analyst breakdowns. Nobody's watching all of that. Here's the actual problem this creates, and the fix.

## The Real Cost of Skipping the Warm-Up Season

Most casual tennis fans show up for the majors and skip the buildup entirely, which means match one of the US Open starts with a context gap: you don't know who's actually in form right now versus who's just seeded high off an older result, you don't know which player quietly had a breakout summer, and you don't know what's actually changed since the last major that would explain why a seed you expected to cruise might not. None of that is available from the bracket alone. It's buried in weeks of coverage that assume you were paying attention the whole time.

The instinct to catch up before the tournament starts is the right one. The instinct to do it by watching hours of preview coverage is where it goes wrong — that's exactly the kind of pre-event catch-up that eats an entire evening for information that's genuinely compressible into a few minutes.

## What Actually Matters Before Match One

**Who's actually in form, not just seeded high.** Seeding reflects ranking points accumulated over a longer window than "who's playing well right now." The players worth watching from match one are often not the top seeds — they're whoever had the better summer, and that's a different list.

**Who's the real dark horse, not just the media-hyped one.** Every major has a "watch this player" narrative pushed hard in preview coverage, and it's not always the player actually positioned to make a run. The players worth knowing about are the ones with a genuine form trajectory, which takes more than a single highlight package to identify.

**What actually changed since the last major.** An injury, a coaching change, a shift in a player's game — this is the context that explains upsets before they happen, and it's exactly the kind of detail that gets buried across scattered warm-up coverage instead of stated plainly anywhere.

## Where AI Recaps Fits Into This Specifically

This is close to the ideal case for summarization: a genuinely large volume of preview and warm-up content, most of which restates the same handful of actual storylines in different words. You don't need all of it. You need the compressed version — the five or six things that are actually true and actually matter heading into match one, pulled from across the coverage instead of requiring you to sit through all of it to find them yourself.

That's a different use case than watching match highlights during the tournament itself, where the actual points matter and a summary is the wrong tool. Pre-tournament context is exactly the kind of content where a summary isn't a compromise — it's the more efficient way to get to the same place.

## The Bottom Line

You don't need to have watched a single warm-up match to walk into the US Open with real context. You need the handful of things that are actually true right now about who's playing well, who's worth watching, and what's changed — compressed into a few minutes instead of scattered across hours of coverage you were never going to watch anyway.
