Every video that lands in your queue deserves one of exactly three treatments: read the summary and move on, watch the whole thing, or skip it outright. Most people default to a fourth option that isn't really an option — leave it in the queue indefinitely, undecided, accumulating guilt. Here's an actual framework for making that call quickly, instead of letting every video sit in limbo.

## The Default Everyone Actually Uses (And Why It's Bad)

Left undecided, most people apply one blanket rule to everything: either "I'll watch it eventually" (the queue grows forever) or "I'll skip anything I don't get to immediately" (you lose things that were actually worth your time). Neither is a real decision. Both are ways of avoiding the actual three-way call, and the queue either grows without bound or gets pruned indiscriminately. A real framework treats each video individually, on its own merits, fast.

## When the Summary Is Enough

**The video's value is informational, not experiential.** If what you actually want is the conclusion, the recommendation, or the key facts — a news explainer, a product review, a "here's what happened" recap — the summary delivers the actual value. Watching the full video adds delivery, not information, and delivery isn't what you came for.

**You're deciding whether something is relevant to you at all.** A lot of videos in a queue aren't really "watch or don't" — they're "is this even about something I care about." A summary answers that question in under a minute, which is exactly the use case where reading beats watching by a wide margin.

**The video is long relative to its actual density.** Plenty of videos pad a small amount of content into a much longer runtime. A summary strips the padding and gets you the actual density in a fraction of the time, without losing anything you'd have valued from the padding in the first place.

## When You Need the Full Watch

**Tone, humor, or delivery is the point.** Comedy, a specific creator's voice, anything where how something is said matters as much as what's said — this is where a summary structurally can't deliver the value, because the value isn't the information, it's the experience of it.

**It's a tutorial or demonstration you're actually going to follow along with.** Watching someone's hands, watching a process unfold in real time — a summary can tell you the steps exist. It can't actually show you them at the pace and detail you need to replicate what you're watching.

**You already know it's good, from a source you trust specifically for full-length value.** Some creators or shows are worth the full watch every time, because you've already established that the format itself — not just the information — is why you follow them. Don't summarize those. Just watch them.

## When It's Actually Fine to Skip

**The summary alone confirms it's not for you.** This is the most underused option. A summary that tells you "this isn't relevant" is doing exactly its job — that's not a wasted minute, that's the fastest possible correct decision. Skipping after a summary confirms disinterest is not the same as skipping out of guilt or backlog panic.

**It's a topic you've already gotten the update on elsewhere.** Redundant coverage of the same story or topic doesn't need a second full watch, and often doesn't need a second summary either, once you recognize the pattern.

## How to Actually Apply This Fast

The framework only works if the sorting decision itself is fast — if deciding which of the three buckets a video belongs in takes as long as just watching it, the framework has failed. In practice, that means leading with the summary almost every time: read it first, and let the summary itself tell you whether this is a "done, that's enough," a "this needs the full watch," or a "skip, no summary needed." The summary isn't just one of the three outcomes. It's usually also the fastest way to figure out which of the three outcomes applies.

## The Bottom Line

A queue full of undecided videos isn't a discipline problem — it's a missing framework. Every video actually sorts cleanly into one of three buckets once you ask the right question: is the value informational (read the summary), experiential (watch it), or already answered (skip it). Applying that in under a minute per video is what keeps a queue from turning into either an ever-growing backlog or a guilt-driven mass deletion.
